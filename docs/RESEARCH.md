# Research Concept: Vehicle-Induced Airflow Harvesting for Supplementary Tunnel Lighting and Monitoring

**Author:** Ranjith Kumar Veesam  
**Date:** 25 October 2026  
**Status:** Conceptual proposal; no experimental results are claimed.

## Abstract

This paper proposes a tunnel-only concept to measure transient airflow created by passing vehicles and assess whether guarded microturbines can supply a useful share of a defined supplementary lighting load. The energy path comprises a turbine array, generator and rectifier, wind-compatible charge controller, protected storage, a metered low-voltage DC bus, and separately fused LED and monitoring branches. A camera and edge-computing subsystem may raise a time- and location-stamped candidate incident for control-room review. The repository provides a transparent first-order power model, an example daily load budget, a generic circuit block diagram, and a reproducible software simulation.

The proposal does not treat traffic wind as free energy. Part of the airflow is driven by vehicle propulsion, and extracting energy can add pressure loss and affect ventilation. Actual net benefit therefore requires concurrent measurements of airflow, generator and delivered electrical energy, ventilation conditions, and any incremental vehicle energy demand. The turbine bus is supplementary and must not be relied on for required tunnel lighting or safety-critical systems. AI outputs do not directly dispatch emergency services or control fans or water suppression. Fire detection, smoke control, and fixed firefighting systems must be engineered and operated as an integrated, tunnel-specific life-safety system.

**Keywords:** road tunnel; traffic-induced airflow; wind energy harvesting; tunnel lighting; energy budget; automatic incident detection; smoke control; safety engineering

## 1. Introduction

Road tunnels require lighting and monitoring while traffic produces short-lived wakes and a longitudinal piston effect. Research has measured these flows and investigated small turbines as energy harvesters. A turbine in the tunnel may be able to contribute power to a small, supplemental DC lighting circuit. Whether it can do so at a useful scale depends on the local speed-time profile, rotor geometry, conversion chain, traffic mix, placement, ventilation strategy, and installation losses.

This concept is scoped to turbines installed inside a road tunnel and their possible contribution to the tunnel's own supplementary lighting and monitoring. It is a standalone project and does not depend on or extend a street-lamp design. The energy and incident-monitoring ideas share a system-level discussion, but the software demonstrator keeps them functionally independent: low turbine output must never disable incident monitoring, required illumination, or emergency services.

The goals are to (1) define an auditable energy boundary, (2) give an illustrative load and output calculation, (3) specify a safe measurement and prototype path, and (4) describe an operator-reviewed incident workflow without inventing live fire-control or emergency-dispatch interfaces.

## 2. Related work and design context

Gallorini and Barelli designed and prototyped a miniature turbine and power-generation section for road-tunnel airflow generated by vehicle movement. This is a direct precedent for testing the idea, but its output belongs to its own turbine and conditions; it is not a guaranteed yield for a different tunnel or array [1]. Łyskawiński, Kowalski, and Wojciechowski experimentally compared Darrieus and Savonius turbine arrangements in passing-vehicle wind. Their measured conditions and turbine results are specific to their test setup [2]. Król and colleagues analyzed traffic-induced tunnel airflow using sensor data, showing why airflow should be measured across real operating periods rather than represented by one peak gust [3].

Tunnel lighting must be designed for visibility, adaptation, daytime and nighttime conditions, maintenance, and failure of normal supply. CIE 088:2004 covers tunnel and underpass lighting considerations and failure of normal power [4]. Harvested energy in this proposal is limited to supplemental loads unless a tunnel-specific electrical, lighting, and safety design proves otherwise.

PIARC describes automatic incident detection as an alert input that can be reviewed by the tunnel control center. It also describes ventilation during a fire as an operational smoke-management strategy tied to tunnel configuration and response plans; simply commanding fans to maximum speed is not a general rule [5,6]. PIARC's fire-detection guidance describes passing parameters to tunnel staff to coordinate traffic control, ventilation, and rescue-service notification [7]. Therefore, this project's AI can only raise a candidate event for human review. Fire mode and suppression decisions belong to approved tunnel systems and procedures.

## 3. Proposed system

### 3.1 Energy subsystem

Place guarded turbines only at locations selected after airflow mapping and tunnel-owner review. Candidate locations may include a protected service recess or other engineered position outside vehicle clearance and evacuation routes. Turbine layout, rotor area, spacing, and mounting must account for flow interaction, structural loading, noise, vibration, debris retention, inspection access, fire behavior, and the pressure drop imposed on the tunnel.

The generator output is rectified and conditioned by equipment designed for the selected turbine and battery chemistry. A protected storage unit feeds a metered low-voltage DC bus. Separate fused branches may serve (a) a defined supplementary LED segment and (b) low-power telemetry if measured surplus is available. Required tunnel lighting, emergency lighting, camera availability required by the operator, fire detection, smoke control, and communications remain on the approved supply and backup architecture.

### 3.2 Incident-monitoring subsystem

An edge camera may analyze traffic for candidate stopped vehicles, collisions, smoke, or fire cues. A candidate event should include camera/zone identity, synchronized time, model and rule version, event category, confidence or score, and a short evidence reference. Operator review and the approved incident plan determine escalation. A vision model trained on general object labels is not by itself a validated crash or fire detector. A tunnel-specific dataset, independent fire inputs, event-level validation, and monitored human review are needed before operational use.

### 3.3 Fire and smoke response

This proposal does not connect AI decisions to exhaust fans, ventilation dampers, water pumps, deluge valves, or dispatch services. Fan direction and speed affect smoke movement and tenability; the correct response depends on tunnel design, fire location, traffic occupancy, escape routes, and the incident command plan. Fixed water-based firefighting also requires integrated fire engineering and approval. A prototype may display a simulated request for operator review, but it must not command these systems.

## 4. Power model and illustrative load budget

For air density ρ, swept area A, and airflow speed v(t), the kinetic power passing through the swept area is approximated as:

P_air(t) = 0.5 × ρ × A × v(t)^3

A simplified DC electrical estimate is:

P_dc(t) = 0.5 × ρ × A × v(t)^3 × C_p(t) × η_g(t) × η_c(t)

where C_p is the turbine power coefficient, η_g is generator efficiency, and η_c is rectifier/controller efficiency. A first-order energy estimate over a day is the integral of output over measured time, summed over turbines. It should be calculated from a measured velocity time series and tested generator output, not from average or peak velocity alone. Battery charge/discharge, standby, wiring, temperature, aging, downtime, and maintenance further reduce delivered energy.

### 4.1 Assumed scenario

This worked example is an arithmetic demonstration, not a performance claim:

| Input | Assumed value |
|---|---:|
| Air density, ρ | 1.2 kg/m³ |
| Turbine swept area, A | 0.25 m² per unit |
| Gust speed, v | 6 m/s during assumed pulse |
| Turbine coefficient, C_p | 0.25 |
| Generator efficiency, η_g | 0.75 |
| Converter efficiency, η_c | 0.90 |
| Pulse duration | 10 s per vehicle |
| Traffic | 1,000 vehicles/day |
| Turbine count | 8 |

For each turbine during a 6 m/s pulse:

P_air = 0.5 × 1.2 × 0.25 × 6^3 = 32.4 W

P_dc = 32.4 × 0.25 × 0.75 × 0.90 = 5.4675 W

Assuming the full 5.4675 W lasts for 10 seconds for each vehicle, one turbine yields 5.4675×10/3600 = 0.01519 Wh per vehicle. At 1,000 identical pulses per day, that is **15.19 Wh/day per turbine**; eight turbines yield **121.5 Wh/day gross**. This multiplication assumes identical, independent pulses for every rotor; real wakes overlap, speeds vary, rotor response is dynamic, and turbine placement can alter flow. The value is therefore only a transparent scenario for sizing discussion.

### 4.2 Example load budget

| Example load | Assumed input | Daily energy |
|---|---:|---:|
| Supplementary LED segment | 12 W × 4 h/day | 48 Wh/day |
| Camera | 5 W × 24 h/day | 120 Wh/day |
| Edge-compute unit | 8 W × 24 h/day | 192 Wh/day |
| **Total** |  | **360 Wh/day** |

The eight-turbine illustrative gross estimate is 121.5 Wh/day, or 33.75% of this assumed total before storage losses, standby consumption, wiring, turbine downtime, and any vehicle-propulsion penalty. It is not enough to claim the array can power the full example system. The energy budget must be replaced by measured tunnel values. If net energy or life-cycle benefit is the research claim, estimate E_net=E_delivered-Δ E_vehicle-Δ E_ventilation-E_standby using a stated system boundary and a matched no-turbine comparison. The incremental vehicle-energy and ventilation-energy terms are unknown here, so net benefit is not calculated.

### 4.3 Battery and load sizing

For a desired autonomy of D days, daily load E_L, usable depth of discharge f_DoD, and delivery efficiency η_s, a first-pass nominal storage estimate is:

E_battery,nominal ≥ D × E_L / (f_DoD × η_s)

This formula omits cold-temperature derating, aging, fault reserve, required redundancy, and code-required emergency operation. A tunnel electrical engineer must size the actual equipment. Safety-critical loads cannot depend on this concept's turbine or example battery.

## 5. Materials and subsystem shortlist

The following list defines categories for a feasibility study, not certified part numbers or a procurement-ready design:

1. Guarded microturbine rotor and engineered enclosure/mounting.
2. Permanent-magnet generator matched to the turbine's speed/torque range.
3. Generator-compatible three-phase bridge rectifier, if the selected generator is three-phase AC.
4. Wind-rated charge controller with overspeed/overvoltage protection and a correctly sized diversion load where the controller requires one.
5. Battery chemistry and BMS selected by the electrical/fire-safety engineer; enclosed and protected installation.
6. Main disconnect, branch fuses/breakers, surge protection, rated DC wiring/connectors, and enclosure protection appropriate to the actual environment.
7. DC energy meter (voltage, current, cumulative Wh), airflow sensor/anemometer, temperature and vibration logging.
8. Supplementary LED driver and luminaire rated for the selected DC bus.
9. Camera and edge-computing hardware only as an independently supplied, operator-approved monitoring subsystem.
10. Independent, approved tunnel fire detection and safety PLC/SCADA interface, designed and installed only by the responsible tunnel integrator.

No electrical ratings, cable gauges, structural fixings, or fire-protection specifications are prescribed here. They depend on site measurements, duty cycle, distance, local electrical codes, environmental classification, and the approved safety design.

## 6. Evaluation plan

1. **Site characterization:** collect traffic counts/classes, speed, existing airflow and pressure, ventilation operating modes, lighting load, temperature, and maintenance constraints over representative periods.
2. **Flow and placement study:** model and measure the tunnel with and without candidate turbine blockage. Assess pressure drop, ventilation effects, acoustic/vibration behavior, safety clearance, and wake interference.
3. **Bench prototype:** test a guarded turbine against realistic gust profiles. Measure torque, rotor speed, generator AC, rectified DC, delivered Wh, startup, overspeed, temperature, vibration, noise, and downtime. Record controller and storage losses.
4. **Limited operator-approved pilot:** connect only an isolated, metered supplementary DC load. Retain existing approved lighting and safety supplies. Compare matched turbine-active and turbine-isolated periods.
5. **Net-energy assessment:** estimate changes in vehicle energy and ventilation energy with an explicit system boundary. Report uncertainty and do not equate measured generator Wh with net savings.
6. **Incident-monitoring validation:** create a privacy-reviewed, labeled tunnel dataset split by time/location. Evaluate event-level precision/recall, false alerts per camera-hour, missed-event categories, and end-to-end latency. Compare AI output with operator-confirmed incidents.
7. **Safety and operations review:** agree test stop limits and response responsibilities with the tunnel owner, fire engineer, electrical engineer, fire service, emergency medical service, and police liaison before any live trial.

## 7. Limitations and conclusion

This is a research concept and simulator, not a completed engineering design or field study. There are no measured airflow profiles, CFD results, turbine prototype data, structural drawings, lighting calculations, qualified battery design, AI training set, medical/police interfaces, or suppression/fan control logic. The example numbers are assumptions used to show calculations. Published turbine studies demonstrate testable approaches, not transferable yields. A site pilot can determine whether a guarded turbine array provides a measurable net contribution to selected supplementary loads without compromising ventilation, safety, or required lighting.
